[Today's Stock] Delisted Japanese Solar Firm Abalance: What's Left for Shareholders After the 199-Yen Final Close

psythinktank_RA|2026.10.02
조회수 33 views閲覧数 3浏览量 3

When a Stock Vanishes From the Exchange, What Happens to the Shares?

Japan's Abalance (3856), formerly known as Realcom, traded for the last time on the Tokyo Stock Exchange Standard Market on September 25, 2026, and was delisted on September 26. As of today (October 2), the exchange no longer publishes a quote. The price used in this article is therefore the 199 yen near the close on the final trading day, September 25. That is roughly 80% lower than a year ago.

The Road to Delisting

  • January 31, 2026: The exchange designated it a Special Alert Market stock (a company that needs to improve its internal management systems).
  • July 31, 2026: It was designated a security on supervision. This is the stage where the exchange reviews whether the listing can be maintained.
  • August 25, 2026: Delisting was decided, and the stock was moved to liquidation trading (the final trading period before delisting).
  • September 25, 2026: This was the last trading day.
  • September 26, 2026: The stock was delisted.

The exchange's reason was that an adequate internal management system was unlikely to be put in place and operated properly. Reports said the improvement plan the company submitted fell short of expectations and showed no sign of a change in approach. The auditor who reviewed the half-year report issued a "disclaimer of opinion." This means the auditor could not obtain enough evidence to judge whether the financial statements were fair. The same opinion was repeated in the following quarterly report and in several amended reports.

According to reports, a third-party committee called for dispersing the authority concentrated in the former chairman and CEO and for separating the domestic and overseas divisions. Management reportedly concluded, after consulting outside experts, that this would be difficult to carry out. Many observers see the failure to fix this concentration of power as the deciding factor.

The Results Looked Good, But...

For the fiscal year ended March 2026, revenue was reported at about 120.2 billion yen (up about 91% year over year) and operating profit at about 13.9 billion yen (up about 446%). Net income also returned to profit. The 104.2% growth rate in the data provided is in line with this top-line expansion. However, with the auditor declining to give an opinion, it has not been confirmed how far these figures can be trusted. It is a case showing that growth numbers and the reliability of the books are separate matters.

Events That Moved the Share Price This Year

  • The stock is reported to have hit a high of 759 yen on January 16 and a low of 184 yen on August 28 this year.
  • In April, the share price fell sharply after an announcement that some cargo had been blocked from import into the United States.
  • On August 4, the stock fell about 19.5%. Issues such as U.S. customs clearance of solar panels from its Vietnamese subsidiary under the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA) were cited as negatives.
  • On July 10, the company announced a settlement of the patent lawsuit brought by JinkoSolar and others.
  • On August 26, sell orders at the daily lower limit piled up after the delisting decision.

What Shareholders Are Left With After Delisting

Delisting does not mean the company disappears. The shares remain, but without the exchange as a trading venue, buying and selling becomes very difficult. As far as I could confirm, neither the exchange nor the company has issued specific guidance on how shares will be handled after delisting. Shareholder return or plans to go private have not been officially confirmed either.

Some people have tried to work out the asset value. One individual blogger estimated the residual value at 460 to 800 yen per share, based on factors such as an indirect stake in Nasdaq-listed TOYO (a solar module maker with a market capitalization of about $187 million as of August 24). But this is only one person's estimate. Unlisted shares are not easy to convert into cash, so there is no guarantee holders will receive the theoretical value.

Summary

This company transformed from a software business into a solar and construction machinery group and was once mentioned as a "ten-bagger" candidate (a stock that rises tenfold). Even such a company can lose its listing if it loses trust in its books and governance. For individual investors, it shows the need to check the audit opinion, progress on improving internal controls, and the degree of management concentration before looking at growth and profit growth rates. It is also worth remembering that liquidity problems after delisting can be especially severe for overseas small-cap stocks.
