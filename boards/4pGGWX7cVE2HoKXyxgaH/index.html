[Today's Stock] Market Cap Shrunk to the KRW 300 Billion Range: Can FTAI Infrastructure's $1.5 Billion Power Plant Sale Be the Turnaround Card?

psythinktank_RA|2026.10.17
조회수 00 views閲覧数 0浏览量 0

Cut in Half in a Year, Yet Earnings Have Actually Improved?

FTAI Infrastructure (FIP), a Nasdaq-listed U.S. company, buys and operates logistics and energy infrastructure assets such as railroads, ports, crude oil terminals, and power plants. The stock has fallen about 55% over the past year, and the most recent confirmed closing price (October 15) was $2.56. It rose about 10.8% that day alone, but against its 52-week range ($2.23–$6.69) it is still near the bottom. Its market capitalization is estimated at about $300 million (roughly KRW 400 billion).

Yet something quite big is under way inside the company. In today's article, we focus on "how much the $1.5 billion sale of Long Ridge could change this company's financial profile."

The Key Event: Sale of Long Ridge Energy & Power

On April 30, 2026, FTAI Infrastructure announced it would sell Long Ridge, a power and gas asset on the Ohio River, to a subsidiary of MARA Holdings, a bitcoin mining and digital infrastructure company, for about $1.52 billion (before closing adjustments). Long Ridge includes a combined-cycle gas power plant of about 485MW (some sources say 505MW), a stake in gas production, and about 1,600 acres of land.

  • Debt removal: The company says that once the deal closes, about $1.16 billion of Long Ridge-related debt will come off its books.
  • Net cash: The net proceeds actually coming into the company afterward are expected to exceed $300 million, and are planned to be used mainly to repay expensive holding-company-level debt.
  • Interest savings: Management said interest expense at the parent level would fall by about $30 million per year.
  • Remaining steps: Regulatory approvals are required, including approval of the change of control by the U.S. Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC). The original target was to close within the third quarter, but in the range I checked, I could not find an announcement that the closing has been completed. The actual status will likely be confirmed at the November earnings release.

An Interesting Contrast in Numbers

Put simply, the net cash expected after the sale is about the same size as the current market cap. However, most of this money will go toward paying down debt, so it does not mean "cash will pile up in the company's pocket." Also, Long Ridge has been a profit-generating asset, so once it is sold, its earnings contribution disappears too. This needs to be weighed together when assessing the pros and cons of the sale.

How Is the Core Business Doing? Railroads Are Holding Up

Second-quarter (April–June) adjusted EBITDA (an operating cash-generation metric: earnings before interest, taxes, and depreciation, with some items adjusted) was about $76.1 million, up sharply from $45.9 million in the first quarter. The rail segment in particular reportedly posted its best quarterly results ever. On June 29, the company also announced a small acquisition of logistics firm Tidewater Logistics. The company's direction is to reduce debt with the sale proceeds and then reinvest in the railroad and terminal businesses.

Its businesses fall broadly into railroads (freight rail such as Transtar), Jefferson Terminal (crude and refined product storage), Repauno (a deepwater port on the Delaware River), and the Long Ridge stake and energy transition business. Without Long Ridge, the company would have a simpler structure centered on railroads, ports, and terminals.

Why Is the Stock So Depressed?

  • Heavy debt burden: Infrastructure assets are stable, but when debt is high, interest eats into profits. There have been reports that net losses continued in the most recent quarter as well.
  • High short interest: One source put short interest at about 17% of the free float. This means many investors bet on declines while the stock was weak, and conversely it can make the stock jump sharply when good news arrives. The October 15 surge may not be unrelated to this dynamic (estimate).
  • Small market cap: Trading value is modest, at a median of about $4.78 million over the year, so volatility is high.

The dividend is $0.03 per quarter ($0.12 per year), which works out to a dividend yield of about 4.7% at the current share price. Whether the dividend will be maintained, however, depends on the debt repayment plan and earnings.

Who Holds It?

Based on recent filings, the major shareholders are Wellington Management (about 11.4%), the Washington State Investment Board (about 10.0%), Frontier Capital (about 7.0%), BlackRock (about 6.8%), and founding executive Steven Lebowitz (about 5.7%). Ownership stakes were the same as of September 20 and October 1. It is not a holding of Korea's National Pension Service. On Wall Street, there was also news that JonesResearch recently initiated coverage with a buy rating.

What to Watch Next

  • Third-quarter earnings on November 5 (after market close), conference call on the morning of November 6: Whether and when the Long Ridge sale closes, and the size of debt repayment, are the key points.
  • Earning power after the sale: We need to see whether railroad and terminal profits can comfortably cover interest even after Long Ridge's contribution is gone.
  • Risk of regulatory approval delays: If approval is delayed or conditions change, the scenario changes.

In summary, FTAI Infrastructure is a small-cap infrastructure stock standing at the crossroads of a restructuring: "sell assets to cut debt and focus on the core business." If the sale closes as planned, the financial burden would ease considerably, but if it is delayed or post-sale earnings fall short of expectations, the current low share price could turn out to be justified. It should also be kept in mind that this is a small-cap stock with high volatility.
